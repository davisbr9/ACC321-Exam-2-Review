Chapter 6: Cash, Receivables, and Notes

Sales discounts, bad debts with the allowance method, and interest on notes that cross year-end.

1. Sales discounts: gross vs. net method

Terms of 2/10, n/30 mean the customer gets 2% off if they pay within 10 days. Otherwise the full amount is due in 30 days. This is a cash discount meant to get people to pay fast.

Don't mix this up with a trade discount, which is just a cut from list price (like 10% off for a new retail customer). Trade discounts never get recorded. You bill the customer net of the trade discount and that's the sale.

Gross method (most common)
Record AR and Sales at the full invoice amount. If the customer takes the discount, debit Sales Discounts.
Net method
Record AR and Sales at invoice minus the discount. If the customer misses the window, credit Sales Discounts Forfeited.

Class example (Example 6.5)

Two sales on account: $4,000 and $6,000, terms 2/10, n/30. The $4,000 customer pays within the discount period. The $6,000 customer pays after it.

Where it shows up on the income statement

  • Sales Discounts (gross method) is a contra-revenue account with a normal debit balance. It's subtracted from Sales Revenue to get net sales: $10,000 - $80 = $9,920.
  • Sales Discounts Forfeited (net method) shows up under Other revenues and gains. Net sales is just the $9,800 Sales Revenue, and the $120 sits below operating income.
Quick check: under the net method, the customer who pays late hands you more cash than the receivable says. That extra is the forfeited discount, so it's a credit.

2. Bad debts: the allowance method

The big risk of selling on account is that some customers won't pay. GAAP wants AR reported at its net realizable value (NRV), which is the amount you actually expect to collect:

Accounts Receivable - Allowance for Doubtful Accounts (contra asset, normal credit balance) = Net realizable value of AR

The direct write-off method (debit Bad Debt Expense, credit AR when an account goes bad) doesn't match expense to the period of the sale and leaves AR overstated. In our class we assume companies can't use it.

Percentage-of-receivables method (the one we use)

  1. Step 1: Figure out the desired ending balance in the allowance: % of AR (or the total from an aging schedule).
  2. Step 2: Bad debt expense is the plug that gets the allowance from its pre-adjustment balance to that desired balance.
Most common mistake: stopping at Step 1 and calling the desired balance the expense. Always do Step 2.

Class example: Hayes Company

At Dec. 31, 2025, before adjusting: AR = $250,000, Allowance = $2,400 (credit, since it's a normal balance). Hayes estimates 10% of AR won't be collected.

Step 1: $250,000 × 10% = $25,000 desired credit balance.
Step 2: $25,000 - $2,400 = $22,600 bad debt expense.

3. Notes receivable that cross year-end

A note is a formal written promise to pay. Interest rates are always annual in our class, so:

Interest = Principal × Annual rate × (Months / 12)

Class example: Cleveland Company

Nov. 1, 2025: Cleveland sells goods to Harrison for $30,000 and accepts a 6-month, 6% note. Year-end is Dec. 31.

  • Total interest: $30,000 × 6% × 6/12 = $900, which is $150 per month.
  • 2025 (Nov. 1 to Dec. 31, 2 months): 2 × $150 = $300 (needs an adjusting entry)
  • 2026 (Jan. 1 to May 1, 4 months): 4 × $150 = $600

Heads up: the class slide text says these are "current liabilities" and later says "Interest Expense." For Cleveland (the lender) they're current assets and the account is Interest Revenue. Looks like a typo on the slide.

Under periodic, the Inventory account still says $30 all year until the adjusting entry.

Perpetual: year-end physical count adjustment

You count inventory at year-end under both systems. Under perpetual, the count almost never matches the book balance, so:

  • Count is less than the Inventory account: debit COGS, credit Inventory for the difference.
  • Count is more than the Inventory account: debit Inventory, credit COGS.

Periodic: the "big" adjusting entry (know the accounts, you won't have to prepare it)

It does three things at once:

  • Moves Inventory from its beginning balance to the ending balance from the count
  • Records Cost of Goods Sold (the number from the schedule below)
  • Closes out Purchases, Freight-In, Purchase Returns and Allowances, and Purchase Discounts

For the $30/$120 example, if the count shows $50 on hand: debit Inventory $20, debit COGS $100, credit Purchases $120.

The big idea: cost of goods available for sale gets split two ways, into ending inventory and COGS. Anything that bumps COGAS up (more purchases, freight-in) or down (returns, discounts) flows into one of those two buckets.

Purchase discounts in the periodic system: we use the gross method (record the purchase at invoice, credit Purchase Discounts if you pay in time). Under the net method, paying late creates Purchase Discounts Lost, reported under Other expenses and losses.

Consigned goods

The consignor is the owner who sends the goods out. The consignee holds and sells them for the owner. The consignor keeps title until the consignee sells to an end customer, so consigned goods are always the consignor's inventory.

Class example: a seed company (consignor) ships seed packets to a store (consignee). The store sells them to you and me, returns the leftovers at the end of the season, and pays the seed company for what sold. The seeds sitting in the store belong on the seed company's books.

3. Cost flow assumptions

The assumption you pick does not have to match how goods physically move. LIFO can't literally happen for a grocery store, and that's fine. It's just how costs get assigned.

  • Specific identification: track the actual cost of each item. Works for a small number of expensive, distinct items. Matches cost flow to physical flow, but can be used to manipulate income (sell the cheaper identical unit first).
  • Average cost: periodic uses one weighted average for the whole period. Perpetual uses a moving average that gets recomputed after every purchase. Hard to manipulate, easy to use.
  • FIFO: oldest costs go to COGS first. Ending inventory sits close to current cost. Doesn't match current costs against current revenue.
  • LIFO: newest costs go to COGS first. Better matching of current costs to revenue. Ending inventory is stuck at old costs. Not allowed under IFRS.

Chapter 8: LCNRV and the Gross Profit Method

Writing inventory down when it loses value, and estimating inventory when you can't count it.

1. Lower-of-cost-or-net-realizable-value (LCNRV)

Why companies do it: once inventory's future usefulness (its ability to bring in revenue) drops below what you paid, reporting it at cost would overstate the asset. So you leave the historical cost principle behind, write it down, and recognize the loss in the period the value actually dropped, not later when it sells.

Estimated selling price - Estimated cost to complete - Estimated cost to sell (disposal, transportation) = Net realizable value

Then for each individual item (that's how we apply it), report the lower of cost or NRV. The total write-down is total cost minus total LCNRV.

Worked example

ItemCostSelling priceCost to completeCost to sellNRVLCNRV
Road bikes48,00060,00005,00055,00048,000
Helmets12,00013,0005002,00010,50010,500
Bike frames30,00038,0006,0003,50028,50028,500
Total90,00087,000

Write-down = $90,000 - $87,000 = $3,000. Notice the bikes' extra $7,000 of NRV doesn't offset the losses on the other items. That's the whole point of the item-by-item approach.

The entry (perpetual system)

Exception: companies using LIFO or the retail inventory method can use lower-of-cost-or-market (LCM) instead. You don't need to know how to do LCM, just that the exception exists.

2. Gross profit method (estimating inventory)

Used when you need an inventory number but can't do a count: interim reports, or inventory got destroyed in a fire or flood.

Class example, Hoover Co.: Inventory Jan. 1 = $150,000, purchases in January = $500,000, sales revenue in January = $700,000, normal gross profit = 35% of selling price.

  1. Cost of goods available: $150,000 + $500,000 = $650,000
  2. If 35% of sales is gross profit, then 65% of sales is COGS: $700,000 × 65% = $455,000
  3. Estimated ending inventory: $650,000 - $455,000 = $195,000
Watch the wording. If the problem says sales are made at "40% over cost" (a markup on cost), convert it first:
GP % of selling price = 40% / (100% + 40%) = 28.571%
Then COGS is 71.429% of sales. Using 40% straight up is the classic trap. Another way to see it: COGS = Sales / 1.40.

Presentation

Companies disclose the composition of inventory, any inventory financing arrangements, and the costing method used, and they have to apply that method consistently from period to period.

Chapter 9 (Part 1): Property, Plant, and Equipment

Acquisition costs, basket purchases, interest capitalization, and selling assets.

1. What goes into the cost of PP&E

PP&E (also called plant assets or fixed assets) is used in operations, not held for resale. It's long-term, usually depreciated, and has physical substance: land, buildings, equipment, furniture, tools.

PP&E is recorded at historical cost, meaning the cash or cash-equivalent price of getting the asset and bringing it to the location and condition necessary for its intended use. Historical cost is reliable because it's based on an actual transaction.

Why costs beyond the purchase price get added

That "location and condition" phrase is the whole reason. Freight, installation, and a test run are all part of what it took to get the asset working, so they're part of its cost. They get spread over the asset's life through depreciation, which matches them against the revenue the asset helps produce. Expensing them right away would understate the asset and dump a big expense into one period.

"Capitalizing" a cost means recording it as an asset on the balance sheet instead of as an expense on the income statement. The cost still becomes an expense eventually, just gradually through depreciation (except land, which isn't depreciated).
AssetCosts that get capitalized
LandPurchase price; closing costs (title, attorney's fees, recording fees); grading, filling, draining, and clearing; assuming any liens, mortgages, or encumbrances; improvements with an indefinite life
Land improvementsLimited-life items like private driveways, walks, fences, parking lots, and landscaping. These are a separate account and are depreciated.
BuildingsMaterials, labor, and overhead during construction; professional fees; building permits
EquipmentPurchase price; freight and handling; insurance while in transit; special foundations; assembly and installation; trial runs
  • Land held for speculation is an investment, not PP&E.
  • Land held by a real estate company for resale is inventory.
  • Self-constructed assets: capitalize materials and direct labor. For overhead, a company can assign none of the fixed overhead, or assign a portion of all overhead to construction. The second way is the most common.

2. Assets acquired for common stock

No cash changes hands, so you need a fair value. When the stock is actively traded, its market value is treated as the cash-equivalent price of the asset.

Class example: Johnson Company

Johnson acquires land by issuing 2,000 shares of $10 par common stock. The stock trades at $40 per share.

  • Cost of land = 2,000 × $40 = $80,000
  • Common Stock (always at par) = 2,000 × $10 = $20,000
  • The rest goes to Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par: $80,000 - $20,000 = $60,000
Land80,000
Common Stock20,000
Paid-in Capital in Excess of Par - Common Stock60,000
If the stock isn't actively traded, use the asset's fair value (like an appraisal) instead, since it's the more reliable number. The idea is to use whichever fair value is more clearly determinable.

3. Lump-sum (basket) purchase

One price buys several assets. Split the total cost among them based on each asset's relative fair value, which is its share of the total fair value.

Recorded cost of asset = (Asset's fair value / Total fair value) × Lump-sum price

Ford pays Samuelson $315,000 for land, a building, and equipment with fair values of $60,000, $220,000, and $80,000.

4. Interest capitalization

GAAP requires a company to add some of the interest it pays during construction to the cost of a qualifying asset. The interest is part of what it cost to get the asset ready for use, which fits the historical cost principle. There are three questions: does the asset qualify, what's the capitalization period, and how much to capitalize.

Qualifying assets

The asset has to require a period of time to get ready for its intended use. Two types qualify:

  • Assets under construction for the company's own use (a plant, a warehouse, special-purpose equipment)
  • Assets intended for sale or lease that are built as discrete projects (a ship, a real estate development)

From the textbook, these don't qualify: inventory produced routinely in large quantities, assets already in use or ready for use, and assets that aren't being used and aren't being worked on (like land just sitting there).

Capitalization period

Begins when all three are true: expenditures for the asset have been made, activities to get it ready are in progress, and interest cost is being incurred. Ends when the asset is substantially complete and ready for use.

Amount to capitalize

Capitalize the lower of:

  1. Actual interest incurred on all debt during the period
  2. Avoidable interest, the interest the company could have avoided if it hadn't spent money on construction

5. Selling PP&E

Book value = Cost - Accumulated depreciation (land isn't depreciated, so its book value is just cost) Gain or loss = Selling price - Book value
Record depreciation up to the date of sale first. If the asset sells partway through the year, record depreciation for those months before you figure book value. Otherwise accumulated depreciation is too low, book value is too high, and the gain or loss comes out wrong.

Class example

Equipment that cost $5,000 with accumulated depreciation of $4,200 sells for $900. Book value = $5,000 - $4,200 = $800. Gain = $900 - $800 = $100.

Cash900
Accumulated Depreciation - Equipment4,200
Equipment5,000
Gain on Sale of Equipment100

The entry always takes the asset off at full cost and clears out all its accumulated depreciation. Cash comes in, and the gain (credit) or loss (debit) balances it.

Income statement effect

Selling PP&E isn't a main operating activity, so a gain goes under Other revenues and gains and raises net income. A loss goes under Other expenses and losses and lowers net income. Neither one touches sales revenue or gross profit.
